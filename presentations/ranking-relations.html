A Framework for Teleological
Relational Egalitarianism
Felipe Agustín Núñez-Michea

Can RE have a teleological structure?

A teleological structure permits at least a partial ranking of outcomes according to a betterness relation.

I adopt an interpretation of relational egalitarianism (RE) as:

  1. a theory of justice,
  2. concerned with the obligations of social and political institutions,
  3. directed at equal civic status in democratic relations free of domination and marginalization.

Citizens hold claims against public institutions. Their satisfaction provides a way to represent and evaluate institutional arrangements.

Civic status: relativities matter

How citizens stand relative to one another, including the absence of hierarchies of domination and marginalization.

The dystopian egalitarian society

Dystopian egalitarian society: (1) In a similar vein to Schelling's segregation model (1971), different groups of society have mild in-group preferences to be close to their own group, ending up, spontaneously, in a segregated society in a finite amount of time.

(2) At least some think of others as mere instruments. The state does nothing to prevent these in-group preferences and dehumanizing beliefs; nevertheless, it secures non-hierarchical relations.

Even without domination or marginalization, the quality of civic relations gives RE a reason to object.

The proposed response: civic status has an absolute dimension as well as a comparative one.

The comparative and absolute dimensions

Relative levels express a comparative relation.

Equal levels can occur when provision is low or when provision is high.

Comparative equality alone leaves the absolute level open.

Absolute equality is about the quality of a relation.

Civic equality as a system of claims

Citizens have claims against public institutions to equal civic status.

A claim has an amount and a strength. Institutional provision determines its degree of satisfaction.

This separates two evaluative concerns:

  • satisfying claims to as large an extent as possible,
  • providing treatment in proportion to claim strengths.

The same representation can support different rules for resolving conflicts between these concerns.

Wintein (2024); Wintein & Heilmann (2024).

Claims and their satisfaction

For one status-conferring good, let citizens be \(I=\{1,\ldots,n\}\).

\[ C_i=(a,\sigma_i), \qquad a>0, \quad \sigma_i>0, \quad \sum_i\sigma_i=1. \]

\(a\) is the common claim amount. \(\sigma_i\) is the fixed comparative strength. Let \(x_i\ge0\) denote effective institutional provision.

Using satisfaction fractions, where \(1=100\%\):

\[ \operatorname{Sat}_i(x_i) = \min\left\{ 1, \frac{x_i}{a} \right\}, \qquad \operatorname{Mat}_i(x_i) = \frac{x_i}{a}. \]

Provision above \(a\) leaves claim satisfaction at \(1\), while mathematical satisfaction continues to increase.

Proportionality and comparative inequality

Strength-adjusted mathematical satisfaction is:

\[ r_i(x) = \frac{ \operatorname{Mat}_i(x_i) }{ \sigma_i } = \frac{ x_i }{ a\sigma_i }. \]

Provision is proportional exactly when \(r_1=\cdots=r_n\).

Sort these values as \(r_{(1)}\le\cdots\le r_{(n)}\). For positive total provision:

\[ L_x^M \left( \frac{m}{n} \right) = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{m} r_{(i)} }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{(i)} }, \qquad m=0,\ldots,n. \]

A higher Lorenz curve means less comparative inequality when the curves are ordered by dominance.

Equal claims: absolute and comparative differences

Take \(a=1\) and \(\sigma_1=\sigma_2=1/2\).

Provision \(x\) Claim satisfaction Comparative profile
\((1/3,1/3)\) \((33\frac13\%,33\frac13\%)\) Equal
\((0.9,0.9)\) \((90\%,90\%)\) Equal
\((0.6,0.3)\) \((60\%,30\%)\) Unequal
\((1,3)\) \((100\%,100\%)\) Unequal

Equal comparative profiles can coexist with different absolute levels.

Full satisfaction can coexist with unequal provision.

Outcome representation and partial ranking

Represent each outcome by:

\[ \mathcal R(x) = \bigl( \operatorname{Sat}(x), L_x^M \bigr). \]

A minimal dominance rule ranks \(x\) above \(y\) if:

\[ \operatorname{Sat}_i(x_i) \ge \operatorname{Sat}_i(y_i) \quad \text{for every }i, \] \[ L_x^M(p) \ge L_y^M(p) \quad \text{for every }p. \]

with strict inequality for at least one citizen or one Lorenz point.

Absolute-first

Prioritize claim satisfaction.

Comparative-first

Prioritize proportionality.

Continuous aggregation

Specify trade-offs between the absolute and comparative dimensions.

1. Lorenz dominance

Same total: \(A=(2,4,6,8)\), \(B=(1,3,5,11)\).
\(A\)'s curve lies everywhere weakly above \(B\)'s and strictly above it somewhere. Therefore \(A\) Lorenz-dominates \(B\).

2. Why use uncapped satisfaction?

Equal strengths: \(\sigma_1=\sigma_2=1/2\), with \(x=(a,3a)\).
Capped satisfaction gives \(\operatorname{Sat}(x)=(1,1)\), but \(\operatorname{Mat}(x)=(1,3)\) and \(r=(2,6)\). Full claim satisfaction therefore does not imply comparative equality.
