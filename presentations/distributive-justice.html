What is needed to formalise a theory of distributive justice?

Distributive justice is about how to allocate goods because of justice reasons.


A theory of distributive justice must specify, among other things, a metric or currency of distributive justice, a pattern governing its distribution, and a scope specifying the domain within which those requirements apply.


Proposed metrics include welfare, resources, primary goods, capabilities, and opportunities.

A particularly illustrative case is to assume that the metric is money. In that case, it is reasonable to represent individual welfare using an increasing function: the more money, the greater the welfare It may also be reasonable to assume that the function is concave, such that the marginal effect of money on welfare is decreasing.

For tractability, well-being is generally used as an umbrella concept that takes the form of a function (see Adler 2019). In this way, the discussion of the metric is generally about the argument(s) of such a function. Given this simplification, pattern is generally defined over well-being: It has been argued, for example, that distributive justice requires maximizing collective well-being (utilitarianism), giving greater weight to the well-being of those who are worse off (e.g., prioritarianism and Rawls’s difference principle), equalizing well-being (egalitarianism), equalizing opportunities to achieve well-being (equal opportunity), or ensuring that everyone achieves a sufficient level of well-being (sufficientism). The list is not exhaustive.

In much of the distributive literature, metric and pattern bear most of the normative weight, while scope is treated primarily as delimiting the domain of application. Let’s call this method ‘top-down’ because it determines metric and pattern independently of the context.
The approach I am describing is Social Welfare Functions Framework (SWFF). This framework abstracts from certain important aspects of the philosophical justification of distributive theories in order to make their structure explicit and facilitate their systematic analysis.
Although SWFF is powerful, it has some important problems, I contend.

1. Its formal assumptions about well-being are substantive.
2. Even if well-being is just an instrumental device, "utility" in the context of SWFF, it seems insufficient as a representational device for reasons that ground justice.
For example, when Daniel says that he should get X because he needs it, has worked for it, or he is entitled to it, he is saying that he has a justice-based reason to have X.

This is not innocent: if Daniel does not get what he claims, he thinks he has suffered an injustice. After all, justice is to give each person what is owed.
It is important to note that it is not specified whether the claim is based on a moral duty, an institutional duty, or something else. Also, it is not specified whether the duty is owed to Daniel himself, nor who owes X. Consider the following cases.
(1) Daniel must receive a present because Ann has a duty owed to him.
(2) Daniel must receive a present because Ann has a duty owed to Daniel’s family.
Let me generalise the idea. A claim is a kind of reason to have something because of a duty. On this, I am following Broome (1991). Nevertheless, I contend that a precise understanding of claims involves four elements: a subject of obligation (O) who must fulfil the directed duty, a recipient (R) of something owed (X), and a claimant (C) to whom the directed duty is owed, and whose standing grounds the claim.
Thus, when Daniel says that he should receive X because he needs it, has worked for it, or he is entitled to it, he invokes a ground of a claim attached to him as R. It remains open who is the claimant C, who is the subject of obligation O, and whether the invoked ground generates a legitimate directed duty at all. For this reason, Daniel’s case rests in a putative claim because the normative assessment of it is a necessary condition that must be satisfied: a putative claim is not a legitimate claim yet. What makes a claim legitimate is a separate question.
If this account of justice as a type of reason in distributive problems is appealing, a comprehensive theory of distributive justice must therefore do at least three things.
The three desiderata: First, a comprehensive theory of distributive justice must specify the structure of claims: who is C, who is O, who is R, and what object X (the metric) is owed. Second, it must define a rule of distribution given the claims (the pattern). Third, it must explain which putative claims are legitimate in a given scope.
Leading theories fail these desiderata because they are largely top-down and they lack the structure of claims.
Just some examples:
Against utilitarianism: Suppose Robert has legally earned and saved $100,000 through a year of being on a budget. A public official discovers that if she secretly takes Robert’s $100,000, she can distribute $10 each to 10,000 people. Each recipient would get a tiny but real benefit: a meal, transport, phone credit, whatever. The total social value is very large because many people benefit a little. Robert suffers a serious loss, but the aggregate gain is greater. Utilitarianism (in its simplest act-utilitarian form) would justify this transfer.
Against prioritarianism: Suppose we must choose between benefiting someone who is rich and someone who is super-rich. Both are already far above any plausible threshold of need, dignity, autonomy, or decent life prospects. Prioritarianism says that, other things equal, a benefit to the merely rich person matters more than an equal benefit to the super-rich person, because the rich person is worse off.

Against sufficientism: Ant and Grasshopper begin with equal resources and capacities. Ant works through the summer and stores food. Grasshopper spends the summer singing, relaxing, or pursuing leisure. Winter arrives; Ant has more than enough, Grasshopper has nothing. Sufficientism holds that Ant should give Grasshopper a share; perhaps a sufficientist state would promote a tax on savings.
I am working on a ‘bottom-up’ possibility; scope is normatively prior: the relations constituting a distributive context partly determine which reasons arise and, consequently, which metrics and patterns are appropriate. (But this is not the purpose of this presentation)
Consider how to satisfy claims (second desideratum). I rely on a recent theory of claims: Wintein & Heilmann (2024a, 2024b), Wintein (2024, 2025). Briefly, claims are objects with two characteristics: amount and strength.
Birthday. It is Ann and Bart's birthday. They separately claim a cake from Carl, arguing that it is their birthday and that he has a duty as a friend to provide it. Carl can afford just one cake. Assuming that both claims are legitimate, what should Carl do?
What is owed is one cake for each. That is, the amount of their claim. The amount of a claim presupposes a satisfaction. Now, because Carl can afford just one cake, there is a comparative question to ask: how must we satisfy their claims? The strength of a claim gives us the relevant information: the strength is a measure of how much, in a distributive problem, a claim weighs.
A problem of distribution can be thought of as a Broomean problem: A Broomean problem (B) is a structure B = <E, N, a, s>, where the estate E > 0 specifies the amount of the good-to-be-divided amongst the individuals in N= {1, … n}. An individual i in N has a claim (ai, si) with amount ai ≥ 0 and strength si > 0, as specified by amounts-vector a and strengths-vector s.

How to satisfy claims in cases of scarcity? How to satisfy them in cases of abundance? The previous machinery helps.
Fair claim satisfaction

Fair claim satisfaction requires:

  1. The satisfaction of claims to as large an extent as possible.
  2. Proportional satisfaction according to strength when compatible with 1.
  3. Treat like cases alike(i), in proportion to the relevant similarities(ii).

These elements can be expressed in terms of axioms. 1. Efficiency expresses efficiency or absolute fairness. 2. Claims-respecting or comparative fairness and P-exactness. 3. Consistency (i) and strength-monotonicity (ii).

Efficiency, claims-respecting, P*-exactness, consistency and strength-monotonicity imply P* rule (see Wintein 2025). Algorithmically: Award each claimant their weighted Proportional division of the estate—unless at least one claimant’s weighted Proportional division is larger than their claim, in which case award such individuals their entire claim, remove them from the set of claimant under consideration and their claim from the estate, and repeat (Wintein 2025, Theorem 1).
